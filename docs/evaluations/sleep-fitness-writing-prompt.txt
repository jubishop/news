NEW STUDY A — CPAP after stroke: the primary heart measurement was inconclusive; a recovery finding merits follow-up.
Primary source, published September 24, 2026, Journal of the American Heart Association. Chou-Han Lin and colleagues. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42786612/ (DOI 10.1161/JAHA.126.050964). The PubMed primary-study abstract was opened and read. The publisher's full text was inaccessible. Do not claim full-text verification.
Randomized trial of 71 people with stroke and obstructive sleep apnea: 35 assigned standard care plus CPAP and 36 standard care alone. Follow-up 12 months. Coprimary measurements included changes and time trajectories of left atrial volume index (a heart-chamber size measure adjusted for body size) and NT-proBNP (a cardiac biomarker). The 12-month between-group difference in change in the atrial measure was 0.5 mL/m2, 95% confidence interval -3.2 to 4.1, P=.804: no clear difference. Different time trajectories were detected, but those do not erase the inconclusive endpoint. Among 63 people completing follow-up, complete neurologic recovery was reported in 46.9% with CPAP versus 22.6% with control (P=.043). This is a secondary/completer finding in a small trial, not definitive evidence that CPAP doubles recovery or prevents another stroke. This abstract does not establish fewer heart attacks, recurrent strokes, or deaths. Adherence details and adverse-event counts were not available in the retrieved abstract; do not invent them. It is not evidence that CPAP is useless for treating sleep apnea.

NEW STUDY B — Choosing a personal peak strength time did not improve the measured training response.
Primary source, first published September 25, 2026, Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle. Fabienne Bruggisser and colleagues. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcsm.70390 . Full paper opened and read, including methods, results and limitations.
Single-centre Swiss randomized trial: 108 adults aged 60–80, mean age 67, 41% women; 93 included in the analyses. Participants were assigned to exercise at their individually measured peak strength time, their trough time, or no training. Peak/trough were identified by testing at 08:00, 12:00, 16:00 and 20:00 over four days. The 12-week program included two supervised resistance sessions and one 30-minute moderate cycling session per week (first two weeks familiarization, then 10 weeks progressive training). Peak versus trough training showed no clear differences in measured maximal pulling strength, grip strength or limb lean mass. Main strength estimate 0.06 N/kg (95% confidence interval -0.76 to 0.88). No intervention-related serious adverse events were reported. Fifteen randomized participants were absent from outcome analyses; the paper reports musculoskeletal complaints among dropout reasons, so do not call the program risk-free. Participants were relatively healthy, high-functioning older adults; do not extrapolate to frail people or competitive athletes. No clear superiority is not proof that timing never matters for every outcome. Practical inference: these results offer little reason to rearrange a workable exercise schedule merely to chase a measured personal strength peak. This trial did not test improved adherence from flexible scheduling.

BACKGROUND C — What a CPAP machine does and practical treatment context (not new evidence).
NHLBI patient information, updated March 24, 2022; opened and read: https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/cpap . CPAP supplies mild air pressure through a mask to keep the airway open during sleep. People may notice less snoring, better sleep quality and less daytime sleepiness. Finding a comfortable mask and adjusting the device can require help from a healthcare provider. Side effects can include nasal congestion, dry mouth and mask irritation. Do not use this general page as evidence that the new stroke trial proved stroke prevention.
