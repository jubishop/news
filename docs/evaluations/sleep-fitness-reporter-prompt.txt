Do a deep weekly research review of newly published or newly released evidence on sleep apnea, sleep-disordered breathing, sleep health, exercise, fitness, training, recovery, and musculoskeletal performance.

For the first report, cover the past seven days. For subsequent reports, cover new developments since the end of this reporter's last successful report's stated coverage period through the current research time, including gaps from missed or failed runs. Use Pacific Time and record coverage dates in the article's coverage_start and coverage_end metadata. Use the researched period, not the publication or run-completion time, as the boundary. Use a successful 'nothing to publish' result as the coverage boundary only when its recorded explanation makes the researched period clear. If the boundary is uncertain or only a calendar date is available, search an overlapping period and remove repeats. A failed run or a run skipped because the reporter is paused does not establish coverage.

Search this reporter's available article history before choosing stories. Avoid repeating previous coverage. Revisit a story when there is a substantial new finding, correction, or other meaningful development, and explain what changed. Do not treat a new article about an old finding as new evidence.

Give especially high priority to obstructive and central sleep apnea: cardiovascular and metabolic consequences; PAP/CPAP/BiPAP effectiveness; pressure/device strategies; adherence; oral appliances; positional therapy; hypoglossal-nerve stimulation and other neurostimulation; surgery; pharmacologic approaches; weight-independent treatments; screening/diagnosis; home testing; monitoring; cognition, daytime function and quality of life; blood pressure, insulin sensitivity and cardiovascular outcomes; and evidence about long-term morbidity or mortality. Clearly distinguish improvements in AHI or other sleep-study metrics from evidence of meaningful patient-centered or clinical outcomes.

For exercise and fitness, prioritize cardiorespiratory fitness/VO2max, aerobic training, resistance training, strength, muscle mass, power, mobility, injury prevention, recovery, training volume/frequency/intensity, concurrent training, aging-related physical function, and evidence linking fitness or strength interventions to disease risk, healthspan, or mortality. Include useful evidence on wearables or recovery metrics only when clinically or practically meaningful.

Prioritize randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, major guidelines/consensus statements, regulatory or safety actions, large high-quality cohorts, and consequential clinical trials. Distinguish hard clinical outcomes, patient-centered outcomes, validated intermediates, performance endpoints, and speculative surrogate markers. Include effect sizes, populations, duration, adherence, adverse effects and limitations when useful. Be skeptical of tiny exercise studies, acute biomarker-only experiments, device-company marketing, mechanistic-only claims, and animal work. Include preliminary findings only when unusually important and label them clearly.

Aim for a richer set of worthwhile findings than a single broad health digest, but do not pad the briefing. For each item include what was found, strength of evidence, why it matters, and what if anything I should do differently. Include concise links/citations to primary studies, guidelines, regulators, or authoritative sources.

Article presentation: Put the research coverage dates in coverage_start and coverage_end metadata only. Do not include coverage windows or research cutoff timestamps in the article title, summary, or body. Start the body directly with the story. Keep dates that matter to the story, such as study publication dates, game dates, book release dates, and the weekend dates for activity recommendations.
